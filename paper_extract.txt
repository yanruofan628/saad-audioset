Real-time brain-controlled selective hearing 
enhances speech perception in multi-talker 
environments
 
Vishal Choudhari

Understanding speech in noisy environments is difficult for many people, 
and current hearing aids often fail because they amplify all sounds rather 
than the talker of interest. Auditory attention decoding (AAD) offers a 
potential solution by using the listener’s brain signals to identify and 
enhance the attended speaker, but it has been unclear whether this can 
provide real-time perceptual benefits. Here we used high-resolution 
intracranial electroencephalography in patients undergoing neurosurgical 
procedures to implement a closed-loop system that achieves the decoding 
fidelity necessary to dynamically amplify the attended talker. Across 
multiple experiments, the system improved speech intelligibility, reduced 
listening effort and was consistently preferred by subjects. It also tracked 
both instructed and self-initiated attention shifts. By providing direct 
evidence that a real-time, brain-controlled hearing system can enhance 
perception, this work establishes a key performance benchmark for future 
auditory brain–computer interfaces and advances AAD from a theoretical 
concept to a validated solution for personalized assistive hearing.
Understanding speech in crowded environments remains one of the 
most difficult challenges in auditory neuroscience and hearing technol-
ogy1. In these situations, listeners rely on selective attention to focus 
on a target talker while suppressing competing voices and background 
noise2,3. Current hearing aids often fail because they cannot infer the 
listener’s intent. As a result, they amplify all sounds indiscriminately, 
leading to degraded performance in real-world settings and contribut-
ing to low user adoption and social isolation1,4.
AAD is a brain–computer interface (BCI) approach that decodes 
a listener’s attentional focus from their neural signals to selec-
tively enhance the attended talker5,6. This approach is grounded in 
foundational work showing that the human auditory cortex produces 
a distinct neural signature for the attended talker7–10. Over the past 
decade, hundreds of studies have focused on improving decoding 
accuracy6,11–14, while only a few have attempted real-time decoding15–17 
and control in closed-loop settings18–20 (Supplementary Table 1). Yet 
one key question has remained unanswered: whether a real-time, 
brain-controlled hearing system can actually help someone hear 
better. The absence of perceptual validation reflects a deeper chal-
lenge. A real-time AAD system must achieve a high-performance 
threshold of speed, stability and accuracy; a system that falls short 
can degrade the listening experience rather than improve it20.

Successfully demonstrating a clear behavioral benefit has therefore 
remained a critical and unachieved milestone for the field of assistive 
auditory neurotechnology.
Here, we address this longstanding gap using high-resolution 
intracranial electroencephalography (iEEG) to establish a 
gold-standard benchmark. We developed a closed-loop AAD system 
that dynamically amplifies the attended talker in real time. We eval-
uated its performance across three complementary experiments: 
(1) testing perceptual benefit when the system is activated mid-trial; 
(2) measuring responsiveness to instructed attention switches; and 
(3) assessing the ability to track natural, self-initiated shifts of atten-
tion. The system significantly improved speech intelligibility, reduced 
listening effort and was consistently preferred over the baseline. By 
providing the first definitive proof of behavioral benefit, this work 
moves brain-controlled hearing from theoretical promise to validated 
technology, establishing a critical performance and viability bench-
mark that confirms the fundamental promise of auditory BCIs and 
laying the foundation for next-generation assistive and augmented 
listening technologies.
Results
Four participants (S1–S4) undergoing epilepsy monitoring with iEEG 
took part in the study (Fig. 1a). All participants had self-reported normal 
hearing. Electrode coverage varied across individuals based on clinical 
needs, with recordings spanning the superior temporal gyrus and sur-
rounding auditory cortex, regions known to encode speech-selective 
neural responses8,21 (Fig. 1b and Extended Data Fig. 1).
To simulate real-world listening conditions, we constructed 
multi-talker scenes containing two simultaneous conversations, spa-
tially separated at ±15° azimuth. Each conversation was a naturalistic 
dialogue between two talkers who took turns speaking about topics 
such as food, travel and exercise. The gender composition of the com-
peting talkers was varied to produce three levels of acoustic similarity: 
different gender (male vs female), mixed gender and same gender 
(male vs male or female vs female), with a balanced number of trials 
for each. To further increase ecological realism, each trial included 
either pedestrian noise or multi-talker babble, randomized across tri-
als. To monitor attentional engagement, both conversations included 
repeated words, but participants were instructed to respond only to 
repeats in the attended conversation by pressing a button. Participants 
S2–S4 were native English speakers; S1 was a non-native speaker.
A two-phase design was used to train and test the system. In the 
offline phase, participants were instructed to attend to a cued talker 
while ignoring the other as their neural responses were recorded. 
Trials were presented with the attended talker set at either +9 dB, 
0 dB or –6 dB target-to-masker ratio (TMR). Behavioral perfor-
mance confirmed that participants reliably tracked the attended 
conversation (Extended Data Fig. 2). Using these data, we trained 
and validated a participant-specific AAD model. The model is a linear 
regression decoder based on a stimulus reconstruction approach5,8,22 
that learns to reconstruct the temporal envelope of the attended 
speech from neural activity. It uses a rich feature set combining 
high-gamma (70–150 Hz) and low-frequency (1–30 Hz) neural enve-
lopes (Fig. 1a and Extended Data Fig. 3) from all speech-responsive 
electrodes (Fig. 1b). The attended speech envelope at each time 
point was reconstructed using the preceding 400 ms of neural 
data. To make a stable decoding decision, the Pearson correlation 
between this continuously reconstructed envelope and the actual 
speech envelopes was then calculated over a 4 s sliding window, 
which was updated with a 0.5 s hop size. AAD accuracy was defined 
as the percentage of windows in which the decoded talker matched 
the cued target. This window size of 4 s was selected based on offline 
performance to balance real-time responsiveness with decoding 
stability (Fig. 1d and Extended Data Fig. 3). Single-electrode decod-
ing performance is shown in Fig. 1c (see Extended Data Fig. 4 for 
subject-level maps). All participants showed decoding accuracy 
well above chance, with average performance ranging from 72.0% 
to 90.3% (Extended Data Fig. 5). This variability could be attributed 
to multiple factors, including subject-specific differences in elec-
trode coverage as well as trial-by-trial variations in a participant’s 
attentional engagement23.
In the subsequent online phase, the frozen AAD model decoded 
attention in real time. To test for generalization across both linguistic 
and acoustic contexts, the speech material used in this phase was differ-
ent, and of the eight talkers presented, four were new to the model and 
had not been included in the offline training set. For each sliding win-
dow, the model reconstructed the attended speech envelope from neu-
ral activity and computed its Pearson correlation with the envelopes 
of the two competing speech conversations5,8. The conversation with 
the higher correlation was assumed to be attended, and this decision 
was used to guide gain control. To prevent abrupt or distracting vol-
ume shifts, we implemented a five-state Markov model that smoothed 
transitions in the TMR24. The system could dynamically shift gain by 
up to ±9 dB, with intermediate steps used to create natural, gradual 
changes. Gain was adjusted by changing the relative levels of the two 
talkers while holding overall power constant (Methods). Instructed 
attention trials used a visual cue (Fig. 1a), and participants were not 
informed that their neural signals were being used to control the audio. 
Depending on the experiment, trials were divided into segments to 
allow comparison between baseline and AAD-enhanced conditions, 
attention-switching periods or natural shifts in focus.
Experiment 1: real-time AAD provides significant and 
multifaceted perceptual benefits
We first tested the perceptual benefit of the real-time AAD system in 
an experiment in which the system was activated mid-trial (Fig. 2a). To 
account for the known build-up of auditory attention, the initial 10 s 
of each trial served as a stabilization period and were excluded from 
analysis (unrated segment, gray bar in Fig. 2a). This 10 s window was 
chosen conservatively, as prior studies have shown that neural atten-
tion tracking typically stabilizes within 3–5 s of stimulus onset25,26. 
Participants then heard a ‘system off’ segment, during which the target 
speech was presented at a challenging −6 dB TMR27. In the following 
‘system on’ segment, the closed-loop system was activated, selectively 
amplifying the neurally decoded attended speech up to +9 dB TMR 
(Supplementary Video 1). At the end of each trial, participants reported 
which segment they preferred and answered two intelligibility ques-
tions probing the target conversation in each condition.
The real-time AAD system accurately tracked attentional focus. 
The resulting single-trial and average correlation differences between 
the neurally reconstructed speech envelope and the envelope of each 
of the two competing conversations showed consistent separation 
between the attended and unattended conversations (Fig. 2b), yielding 
an average attention decoding accuracy that was significantly above 
chance level for all subjects (Fig. 2c). This effective neural decoding 
directly translated into the system’s gain control; a trial-by-trial analysis 
confirmed that average AAD accuracy in each trial was a significant 
predictor of the resulting average TMR (adjusted R2 = 0.98, P < 0.001; 
Extended Data Fig. 6), leading to an average TMR boost of +12 dB 
between the ‘off’ and ‘on’ segments (Fig. 2d).
This brain-controlled gain change led to clear and multifaceted 
perceptual benefits. Participants demonstrated a strong preference 
for the listening experience when the system was active. A generalized 
linear mixed-effects model (GLMM) on trial-level ratings, with subjects 
as random effects, revealed a highly significant preference for the ‘sys-
tem on’ condition (estimate = 1.55, SE = 0.29, P < 0.001), with individual 
preference rates for the ‘system on’ condition ranging from 75% to 95% 
(Fig. 2e). This subjective benefit was mirrored by improved objective 
performance: a GLMM on the question–answer responses showed that 
when intelligibility changed between segments, it was significantly

Crucially, we found that fluctuations in decoder fidelity had a 
direct and meaningful impact on the user’s subjective experience. A 
trial-level GLMM analysis revealed that single-trial AAD accuracy was 
a highly significant predictor of user preference (estimate = 0.042, 
SE = 0.013, t78 = 3.36, P = 0.0008). This positive coefficient indicates

For example, in trials in which the system achieved high accuracy 
(>75%), participants consistently preferred the system, whereas pref-

This confirms that achieving high-fidelity neural decoding, whether 
through better models or higher quality signals, is not merely a tech-
nical objective, but is essential for delivering a consistently beneficial 
and preferred user experience. Together, these results provide behav-
ioral and physiological evidence that real-time, brain-controlled gain 
modulation can reliably improve the overall listening experience in 
challenging acoustic environments.
Experiment 2: the system tracks instructed attention switches
In real-world conversations, listeners often shift attention between talk-
ers. A key concern for closed-loop AAD systems is whether amplifying 
the currently attended talker in real time might hinder the listener’s 
ability to switch attention to another, now less audible talker. Specifi-
cally, we wondered whether users still switch focus, and whether the 
system can detect and adapt to the new talker with sufficient speed to 
support a seamless experience. Our next experiment tested whether 
the AAD system could track such instructed attention switches and 
dynamically update the gain in real time.
In each trial, participants were initially cued to attend to one of 
two overlapping speech streams. Midway through the trial, a visual 
cue instructed them to switch their focus to the other stream, while 
the system remained active throughout (Fig. 4a and Supplementary 
Video 2). The system successfully tracked this shift in focus. As shown 
in Fig. 4b, single-trial and average correlation differences exhibited a 
clear transition in neural tracking following the switch cue, resulting 
in a successful decoding of attentional focus (Fig. 4c). Given that the 
decoding output controls gain dynamically, the system’s TMR shifted 
accordingly, favoring the first conversation before the switch and 
the second conversation after it (Fig. 4d). We quantified the system’s 
responsiveness using ‘switch time’, defined as the time required for 
the average TMR to reach 0 dB following the switch cue. Across par-
ticipants, the mean switch time was 5.1 s (S1, 7.0 s; S2, 4.5 s; S3, 5.0 s; 
S4, 4.0 s). This latency reflects both the listener’s cognitive response, 
but more prominently, the system’s design choices, including the 4 s 
sliding window and the five-state Markov model that smoothed gain 
transitions to ensure perceptual stability.
To confirm that participants successfully followed the instructed 
switch, we analyzed their performance on the repeat-word detection 
task. The results, visualized in Fig. 4e, show a clear interaction. The plot 
displays the overall mean performance for each condition (bars) and 
the average trend for each individual subject (colored lines). A clear 
‘X’ pattern is visible: performance for conversation 1 (attended first) 
is high pre-switch and drops after the cue, while performance for con-
versation 2 (attended second) is low pre-switch and rises after the cue.

analysis. The model included fixed effects for the trial segment (pre vs 
post switch), the conversation (conversation 1 vs conversation 2) and,

Experiment 3: the system successfully tracks self-initiated 
attention switches
In natural listening, attention shifts are often internally driven rather 
than externally cued31. This experiment tested whether the AAD system 
could detect and respond to such self-initiated shifts in attentional 
focus, without any external timing signal (Fig. 5a). Participants freely 
redirected their attention between two competing talkers while the 
system remained active throughout. To provide a behavioral refer-
ence, participants indicated their current focus by pointing to the 
loudspeaker they were attending to. Although this involved a motor 
gesture, the signal driving the decoder was based on speech envelope 
tracking, and we confirmed that the motor behavior was uncorrelated 
with the speech envelopes and therefore could not systematically influ-
ence the decoding process (Extended Data Fig. 8).
The system successfully tracked voluntary shifts in attention. 
As shown in Fig. 5b, single-trial and average correlation differences 
between the reconstructed and actual speech envelopes exhibited 
clear transitions aligned with participants’ self-reported focus. The cor-
responding TMR adapted in real time, enhancing the newly attended 
stream while attenuating the previously attended one (Fig. 5c). These 
results demonstrate that real-time AAD can flexibly and accurately 
follow endogenous changes in attention without requiring external 
cues (Supplementary Video 3).
To further test the system’s perceptual impact, we introduced a 
control condition in which gain control was intentionally reversed with-
out informing the participant. In this condition, the system amplified 
the unattended speaker and suppressed the attended one. Participants 
immediately reported increased difficulty and a degraded listening 
experience (Supplementary Video 4). This condition serves as a critical 
control: if correct AAD-based gain improves perception, then revers-
ing it should, and did, disrupt it. This further confirms the behavioral 
relevance and robustness of attention-driven enhancement.

Discussion
Establishing a benchmark for brain-controlled hearing
This study addresses a critical and longstanding gap in auditory BCIs by 
providing the first direct evidence that a real-time, closed-loop AAD sys-
tem can improve speech perception in multi-talker environments. This 
work represents a key milestone, demonstrating that brain signals can 
be used not only to decode attentional focus but also to enhance intel-
ligibility, increase subjective preference and reduce listening effort, 
advancing AAD from a theoretical concept to a validated perceptual 
technology. By showing that the system adapts to both instructed and 
self-directed attention shifts, we establish its flexibility in dynamic 
listening conditions, an essential requirement for real-world usability. 
Finally, the finding that the same brain-controlled output enhanced 
comprehension in individuals with hearing loss highlights the system’s 
translational potential.
A key aspect of this study was the use of high-resolution intracra-
nial recordings, which provided the signal quality needed to answer 
the central question of whether a brain-controlled hearing system can 
yield behavioral benefits. Although iEEG is not an immediate practical 
solution for widespread use, it was a scientifically necessary choice to 
establish a gold-standard benchmark for the field. This approach is 
consistent with other grand challenge problems in BCI research, includ-
ing recent landmark studies in speech prosthesis development35–38. 
Our findings define an upper bound on AAD performance—what is 
achievable when attentional intent is decoded with high fidelity—and 
offer a reference point for future, less invasive systems that can be 
scaled to larger populations. At the same time, the neurotechnology 
landscape is evolving rapidly toward implantable devices for a variety of 
neurological and psychiatric conditions, including epilepsy39, depres-
sion40 and movement disorders41. Given that disabling hearing loss 
negatively affects more than 430 million people worldwide42, a safe,

Validation in listeners with hearing loss
Our supplementary validation study provides important evidence 
for the clinical potential of this technology. Participants with hearing 
impairment listened to audio that had been dynamically modulated 
in real time based on the neural signals of a separate user, resulting in 
moment-by-moment gain adjustments. These brain-driven modula-
tions are inherently variable and would not necessarily improve per-
ception if they were unstable or mistimed20. The finding that listeners 
with hearing impairment not only preferred the brain-driven audio 
but also derived a greater objective benefit than individuals with nor-
mal hearing is a significant outcome. Consistent with this finding, we 
observed that objective intelligibility gains were most pronounced for 
individuals with moderate-to-severe hearing loss, whereas participants 
with mild hearing loss who operated near the ceiling of performance 
showed smaller gains, even though they consistently preferred the 
system-enabled condition. The feasibility of a future closed-loop 
system for this population is supported by prior work establishing 
that the necessary neural signatures for AAD are robustly present in 
hearing-impaired listeners18,33,34, and that decoding can remain effective 
even when speech intelligibility is severely degraded23,43,44. Together, 
these findings suggest that closed-loop neural decoding systems are 
viable for this population and may offer the greatest objective benefit 
to those with the most to gain. As such, they represent a promising 
direction for the future of assistive hearing technology.
Real-world viability with mixed audio
A critical prerequisite for the translation of any AAD-based hearing 
device is the ability to operate on mixed audio signals, as clean source 
signals are unavailable in real-world environments. Although this has 
long posed a barrier for the field, recent breakthroughs in real-time 
speech separation45–49 have made this goal increasingly achievable. 
Prior work has shown that AAD can be integrated with low-latency sepa-
ration algorithms to operate on mixed audio without a significant loss 
in decoding accuracy12–14,50–53. In the present study, we used separated 
sources strategically to isolate and definitively answer the more funda-
mental question of whether a brain-controlled system could provide 
behavioral benefit. To confirm the broader viability of this approach, 
a control analysis showed that decoding performance was virtually 
unchanged when using algorithmically separated sources46 instead 
of clean sources (r = 0.97, P < 0.001; Extended Data Fig. 10). Neverthe-
less, significant challenges remain in ensuring robust performance in 
complex, real-world conditions, including highly reverberant spaces 
and scenes with multiple competing talkers54. In such scenarios, speech 
extraction, focusing on enhancing a target speaker rather than fully 
separating all sources, may offer a more practical and computationally 
efficient solution51. The translational potential for AAD-based sys-
tems is further supported by ongoing progress in deploying machine 
learning-based enhancement methods on modern, power-efficient 
hearing aid hardware, making real-time implementation of AI-driven 
auditory interfaces increasingly feasible55.
Neural mechanisms and decoding strategies
In this study, we deliberately used a simple linear decoding model5,22, 
chosen for its computational efficiency, transparency and robust-
ness in real-time closed-loop operation. Given the high signal-to-noise 
ratio of iEEG recordings, this approach allowed us to reconstruct the 
speech envelope from broadband neural responses and compare it to 
candidate streams using Pearson correlation, a well-established and 
interpretable framework in auditory neuroscience. Using a lightweight 
model also minimized the risk of instability or unintended behavior 
in the feedback loop, ensuring that any observed perceptual benefit 
could be directly attributed to attentional decoding. Our results further 
showed that trial-by-trial variations in AAD accuracy were significantly 
correlated with user preference, reinforcing the importance of decoder 
performance for perceptual outcomes. To rule out non-auditory con-
founds, we confirmed that neither the visual cue (experiment 2) nor 
the motor pointing behavior (experiment 3) correlated with the actual 
or reconstructed speech envelopes (Extended Data Fig. 8). Moreo-
ver, most behavioral outcomes were collected in experiment 1, dur-
ing which no motor input was present and the visual arrow remained 
fixed throughout each trial. Finally, we observed that informative elec-
trodes were not restricted to the left superior temporal gyrus but were 
also found in the right hemisphere and dorsal frontoparietal regions 
(Fig. 1c). This suggests that the linear decoder can leverage signals from 
a distributed cortical network, probably recruiting higher-order atten-
tional resources in addition to sensory processing, which contributes 
to the robustness of the system.
Building on the importance of decoder fidelity, nonlinear and 
end-to-end models have shown clear potential for improving decod-
ing performance and speed, particularly in lower signal-to-noise ratio 
environments or more spatially constrained recording settings12,34,56–62. 
However, we note that more complex nonlinear models often incur 
higher computational costs, which could increase system latency to a 
point that diminishes the behavioral benefit. Other approaches have 
also emerged, such as decoding based on distinct neural population 
responses rather than temporal correlation between stimulus and 
response63. These methods may help extend AAD to a broader range of 
signal modalities, including emerging minimally invasive and wearable 
technologies that fall between traditional invasive and non-invasive 
categories64. Theoretical work further suggests that current AAD 
algorithms operate below information-theoretic limits65, pointing to 
substantial headroom for future gains.
System latency and stability constraints
A deeper understanding of how decoding accuracy, latency and model 
complexity translate into perceptual benefit will be essential for guid-
ing system design and enabling clinical translation. For example, the 
5.1 s switching time observed in our attention-switching task is not a 
neurophysiological limit8, but a direct consequence of parameters 
chosen to prioritize stability: a 4 s decoding window contributes an 
inherent 2 s latency, and a five-state Markov smoothing filter adds 
more delay to the transition time. This illustrates the fundamental 
speed–accuracy trade-off that must be carefully optimized in any 
practical AAD system. The implementation of such systems must also 
carefully balance speech enhancement with environmental awareness. 
Our findings suggest that an approximate gain adjustment of +6 dB in 
TMR significantly improves intelligibility without fully isolating the 
listener from their surroundings. However, real-world applications 
will introduce additional complexities, including complex sound 
scenes with non-speech sources and unpredictable attention shifts66,67. 
Addressing these challenges through further experimental and com-
putational refinement will be vital for successful deployment. We also 
acknowledge that the fixed trial structure in experiment 1 (‘system off’ 
followed by ‘system on’) introduces a potential confound regarding 
attentional build-up. Although we excluded the initial 10 s to account 
for the rapid formation of auditory streams, it is possible that reduced 
listening effort in the second segment partially reflects temporal adap-
tation to the task. However, given the substantial magnitude of the 
improvement in both intelligibility (+12 dB TMR boost) and subjective 
preference, we attribute the primary benefit to the system-driven 
enhancement.
Neural decoding overcomes the limits of external cues
A natural question is whether simpler external cues, such as eye-gaze, 
head orientation or manual gestures, could provide the same steering 
information without neural sensing. Although these external cues

can be useful in specific scenarios, each has fundamental limitations 
that a direct neural interface overcomes. Gaze direction, for exam-
ple, fails when competing talkers are co-located, when there is no 
visual target accompanying the listening target (for example, visual 
occlusion or a public-address system). Furthermore, gaze and audi-
tory attention routinely dissociate; listeners often covertly attend to 
one talker while looking elsewhere68. Moreover, as listening becomes 
effortful, eye movements decrease and spatial gaze width narrows, 
increasing the difficulty of inferring where attention is deployed from 
gaze69. In live conversations, gaze also serves social functions, regulat-
ing turn-taking70, reflecting one’s speaking and listening role, which 
changes with group size71, further weakening any mapping from gaze 
direction to attentional focus. The presence of individual gaze differ-
ences adds another layer of unpredictability72. Related external cues 
face similar issues: head pose and pointing gestures are constrained by 
visibility, social norms and task demands, and they impose an explicit 
behavioral burden that is fatiguing and ill-suited to rapid turn-taking. By 
contrast, neural attention decoding is covert, continuous and agnostic 
to spatial arrangement; it seamlessly follows the listener’s internal focus 
even when gaze and head pose provide no reliable spatial cue, and it 
requires no behavioral modification in addition to ordinary listening. 
Moreover, brain signals carry information about higher-order listening 
goals—what stream is semantically relevant and what sounds are to be 
suppressed—that eye or head trackers cannot access. Decoding those 
higher-level intentions opens a path toward future devices that not 
only amplify the currently attended talker but also can flexibly remix 
complex soundscapes73. By demonstrating that brain-based steering 
delivers a perceptual benefit under realistic conditions, we lay the criti-
cal groundwork for next-generation assistive technologies that adapt 
to the listener’s true cognitive intent rather than to an observable, and 
often unreliable, surrogate.
Subject experience and system perception
In addition to the quantitative behavioral metrics, participant feed-
back provided valuable insights into the usability of the closed-loop 
system (Supplementary Video 5). In post-experiment debriefings, 
users consistently described the adaptive gain control as feeling 
natural and intuitive, noting that they could shift their attention 
freely without perceiving the system as resistant or lagging. Cru-
cially, participants reported that the enhanced audio aligned well 
with their intended focus, reinforcing the objective finding that the 
decoder accurately tracked their attentional state. These qualitative 
reports suggest that despite the inherent technical latency of the 
system, the perceptual experience remained seamless, a critical fac-
tor for the long-term adoption of any future neuro-steered hearing 
technology.
This work also opens broader avenues for integrating cognitive 
states into assistive hearing technologies, shifting the paradigm from 
passive amplification to active, personalized, intent-driven auditory 
enhancement. By establishing the critical performance and viability 
benchmark for a functional auditory BCI, this study provides a prin-
cipled framework for designing future systems that may incorporate 
additional cognitive dimensions, such as perceived effort, listening 
intent or situational context, enabled by emerging AI frameworks that 
fuse neural decoding with adaptive machine listening63. By aligning 
auditory processing more closely with the user’s internal goals, this 
approach offers a path toward more intuitive, responsive and effective 
hearing support in everyday environments.

Methods
Inclusion and ethics
All participants provided informed written consent before partici-
pation in accordance with protocols approved by Columbia Univer-
sity’s Institutional Review Board and the respective hospitals’ ethics 
committees. Participants in the iEEG experiments were adult patients 
undergoing clinical monitoring for epilepsy with implanted intracranial 
electrodes. The study adhered to all relevant ethical regulations and 
guidelines for research involving human participants.
Subjects
Four participants (S1–S4; three males aged 24, 29 and 38 years and one 
female aged 21 years) who were undergoing clinical monitoring for 
epilepsy with implanted intracranial electrodes (iEEG) were recruited 
for this study, following approval by the institutional review boards of 
the participating hospitals. No financial compensation was provided 
for participation in the experiments. Informed consent was obtained 
from all participants before enrollment. Electrode coverage varied 
across individuals. Subjects S1 and S3 had stereoelectroencephalog-
raphy (sEEG)-depth electrodes and an electrocorticography grid over 
the left hemisphere, primarily targeting the temporal lobe. Subject S2 
had sEEG-depth electrodes in the right hemisphere, while subject S4 
had bilateral sEEG-depth coverage. Speech-responsive electrodes were 
identified based on neural activity recorded during the offline phase. 
High-gamma power (70–150 Hz) was extracted from each electrode, 
and speech responsiveness was assessed using t-tests comparing activ-
ity during speech versus silence. Based on this analysis, the number of 
speech-responsive electrodes used in subsequent analyses for each 
participant was 48 for S1, 38 for S2, 42 for S3 and 21 for S4.
For evaluation in individuals with hearing loss, 40 participants 
were recruited through the online platform Prolific. Participants were 
compensated USD $10 for approximately 30 min of participation. All 
participants were native English speakers. The mean age of participants 
was 40 years (s.d. = 13 years), with an age range of 20–73 years. The 
cohort included 25 male participants.
Experimental design and stimuli
The experiment consisted of two phases: an offline phase to collect 
training data for the AAD model, and an online phase in which the 
trained model was used in a real-time closed-loop setting. In both 
phases, participants listened to two spatially separated conversations 
presented simultaneously through external loudspeakers positioned at 
±15° azimuth. Each conversation comprised scripted dialogue between 
two speakers who took turns discussing everyday topics (for example, 
food, travel), recorded by professional voice actors to ensure natural-
istic but controlled stimuli.
To control acoustic difficulty, trials varied in the gender composi-
tion of the competing talkers. Talker pairs were selected to produce 
three levels of overlap: easy (male vs female), medium (mixed-gender 
across conversations) and hard (same-gender talkers). The distribution 
was balanced across the experiment. All dialogues were matched for 
duration and loudness across conditions. To simulate realistic acoustic 
environments, diotic background noise was added to each trial, using 
either pedestrian noise or multi-talker babble (randomized across tri-
als). The background noise level was set 15 dB below the target speech 
in trials with 0 dB TMR and remained constant across phases. Each trial 
also included a one-back task: repeated words were inserted into both 
conversations, and participants were instructed to press a button when-
ever they detected a repetition in the attended stream. This ensured 
engagement and compliance with attentional instructions. A central 
display provided visual cues (an arrow) to instruct participants which 
stream to attend. In experiment 1, this cue remained static throughout 
the trial, whereas in experiment 2, it changed mid-trial to signal an 
attention switch. Additionally, in experiment 3, participants used a 
hand gesture to indicate their attentional focus for post hoc analysis. 
A potential concern is that neural activity related to these visual or 
motor events could contaminate the auditory decoding. However, this 
is theoretically implausible, as our stimulus reconstruction decoder is 
trained to reconstruct the temporal envelope of speech, a signal that is 
not temporally correlated with the change in visual cue (experiment 2) 
or motor action (experiment 3). We confirmed this empirically by 
showing a lack of significant correlation between the visual cue signal 
and the speech envelopes (P > 0.5; Extended Data Fig. 8).
To equalize loudness across trials and talkers, individual speech 
recordings were first normalized to a root-mean-square amplitude of 
1.0, concatenated into full conversation streams and then scaled to 
−25 dBFS to prevent clipping and maintain consistent signal power. 
The final mixed audio was presented at 65 dB sound pressure level. In 
real-time conditions, the gain of each stream was dynamically adjusted 
to implement the desired TMR, while total acoustic power was held 
constant across transitions.
Offline phase: data collection and model training
The offline phase was designed to collect labeled training data for 
subject-specific AAD models. Participants completed 26 trials, each 
lasting approximately 38 s. To ensure the decoder would generalize 
across a range of signal conditions, the trial order was randomized 
across three TMR configurations: in ten trials, the TMR shifted from 
−6 dB to +9 dB; in another ten trials, it shifted from +9 dB to −6 dB; 
and in the remaining six trials, the TMR was held constant at 0 dB. At 
the start of each trial, a visual arrow cued the participant to attend 
to one of the two conversations, and no attentional switches were 
prompted. For analysis, data from trials with TMR changes were divided 
into two TMR-specific segments, while trials without TMR changes 
were treated as a single segment. Neural data were normalized across 
all segments, whereas stimulus envelopes were normalized within 
each segment. Decoders were then trained and evaluated using a 
leave-one-segment-out cross-validation procedure. Throughout this 
phase, participants performed the one-back repeat-word detection 
task, and their behavioral performance confirmed they reliably tracked 
the attended conversation (Extended Data Fig. 2).
Online experiment
The AAD model trained during the offline phase was deployed to 
decode attention and enhance the attended conversation in real time. 
Participants were informed that the online phase was a continuation 
of the listening task but were not told that their brain activity would 
control the system, ensuring a blinded experimental design. The 
online phase included three types of trials: binary forced-choice tri-
als (experiment 1), instructed attention-switching trials (experiment 2) 
and self-initiated attention-switching trials (experiment 3).
Experiment 1: binary forced-choice trials. Participants completed 
20 trials, each lasting 38 s. The initial 10 s of each trial served as an 
unrated stabilization period to allow for the build-up of auditory atten-
tion. Following this period, an on-screen prompt explicitly marked the 
beginning of ‘segment 1’ (the 14 s ‘system off’ period), during which the 
TMR was fixed at −6 dB. At the 24 s mark, a second prompt marked the 
beginning of ‘segment 2’ (the 14 s ‘system on’ period), during which the 
closed-loop system was activated, dynamically amplifying the attended 
talker by up to +9 dB TMR. At the end of each trial, participants were 
asked to rate which of these two explicitly marked segments was easier 
to follow and answered two multiple-choice comprehension questions. 
Throughout the trial, they also performed the one-back repeat-word 
detection task.
Experiment 2: instructed attention-switching trials. In ten additional 
trials, participants were cued to shift attention from one talker to the 
other midway through each trial. A continuously displayed arrow on 
the central screen indicated the cued talker and flipped direction at a

predetermined time. Trials lasted 38–47 s. Speech began at 0 dB TMR, 
and closed-loop gain control was activated 5 s after onset to allow 
model stabilization before the switch.
Experiment 3: self-initiated switching and control condition. In a 
third condition, participants were allowed to switch attention between 
talkers freely and at will, without any external cue. To monitor atten-
tional state, they pointed to the loudspeaker they were attending to, 
providing a behavioral reference for evaluating the decoder. These 
trials tested whether the system could track endogenous attention 
shifts and dynamically adjust enhancement in real time.
As a control condition, some of these trials included a reversal 
of the gain logic: the system was configured to amplify the unat-
tended talker and suppress the attended one, without informing 
the participant.
At the end of the session, participants were debriefed and informed 
that their brain activity had controlled the gain modulation in real time. 
They were asked about their experience and whether they would con-
sider using such a system in real-world listening scenarios.
Regression analyses of performance and preference
To understand the relationship between user engagement, system 
performance and subjective preference (Fig. 3), we performed two 
complementary regression analyses using mixed-effects models 
to account for within-subject correlations. First, to investigate the 
source of trial-by-trial variability in system performance (Fig. 3a), we 
used a linear mixed-effects model. We modeled single-trial AAD accu-
racy as the dependent variable, with the participant’s repeat-word 
detection score (a proxy for attentional engagement) as the fixed 
effect. A random intercept for each subject was included to account 
for individual differences in baseline decoding performance. Second, 
to quantify the impact of system performance on subjective prefer-
ence (Fig. 3b), we used a GLMM. The binary preference outcome 
(prefer on vs prefer off) was modeled as a function of single-trial AAD 
accuracy (fixed effect), with a random intercept for each subject. The 
model specified a binomial distribution with a logit link function. For 
visualization purposes in Fig. 3b, trials were grouped into three bins 
based on AAD accuracy. We calculated the proportion of ‘prefer on’ 
responses within each bin (mean ± s.e.m.) to display the empirical 
probability of preference across the accuracy range without impos-
ing a sigmoidal curve fit.
Pupillometry
Pupil size was used as a physiological proxy for listening effort, a 
well-established metric in auditory neuroscience23,28–30. Larger pupil 
dilations are associated with increased cognitive load, while smaller 
dilations reflect reduced effort. In the context of AAD-driven speech 
enhancement, we reasoned that pupil dilation would be lower when 
the system successfully enhanced the attended stream (that is, under 
positive TMR conditions) compared to trials in which the system was 
inactive or misaligned (negative TMR).
Pupil diameter was recorded using a Tobii Pro Nano eye tracker 
(60 Hz sampling rate) positioned at chin level beneath the display. 
Raw traces from the left and right pupils were averaged and aligned 
to the audio onset. Baseline correction was performed for each 
trial by computing the mean dilation during the 1 s period preced-
ing trial onset and rescaling the pupil data accordingly. Reliable 
pupillometry data were obtained from two participants (S2 and S3) 
across all 20 binary forced-choice trials. Data from the remaining 
two participants (S1 and S4) were excluded from analysis because 
of technical limitations in eye-tracker positioning that prevented 
consistent tracking.

Study description
This study employed a quantitative, within-subject experimental design to assess behavioral and neural correlates of auditory 
attention in participants undergoing intracranial EEG monitoring. iEEG Participants completed controlled listening tasks while neural 
signals were decoded in real time to modulate auditory stimuli. Then, an independent online cohort of individuals with hearing loss 
was used to listen to the brain-modulated stimuli and evaluate behavioral outcomes in the hearing loss group.
Research sample
The research sample consisted of four adult epilepsy patients (three males and one female; ages 21–38 years) undergoing clinical 
intracranial EEG monitoring as part of their medical care. All participants self-reported normal hearing. One participant was a non-
native English speaker; the remaining participants were native English speakers. The sample was selected based on clinical availability 
and electrode coverage and is not intended to be representative of the general population. 
 
An additional cohort comprised 40 adults with self-reported hearing loss recruited online through Prolific (25 male, 15 female; ages 
20–73 years; mean age = 40 years, s.d. = 13). All participants were native English speakers and completed behavioral testing 
remotely.
Sampling strategy
iEEG participants were recruited using convenience sampling from patients undergoing clinical intracranial monitoring at partner

Data collection
Data collection procedures differed across cohorts. iEEG data were collected in hospital settings using implanted intracranial EEG 
electrodes, audio presentation systems, and eye-tracking equipment. Participants completed structured listening tasks while neural 
and behavioral responses were recorded. No individuals other than the participant and research staff were present during data 
collection. Participants were not informed during real-time sessions that neural decoding was actively modulating audio stimuli. 
 
Behavioral data from the hearing-loss cohort were collected remotely via an online listening experiment completed using 
participants’ own PCs.
Timing
iEEG Data were collected during participants’ clinical monitoring periods between 2023 and 2024. Each participant completed 
multiple experimental sessions during their hospital stay. Online behavioral data from hearing-loss participants were collected during 
the same overall study period.
Data exclusions
No behavioral or neural data were excluded based on performance. Pupillometry data from two participants (S1 and S4) were 
excluded due to eye-tracker calibration issues and were not pre-specified for exclusion. All other data were analyzed as collected. No 
additional exclusions were applied to the hearing-loss cohort.
Non-participation
No participants declined participation or withdrew from the study after enrollment.
Randomization
Participants were not randomly assigned to experimental conditions, as the study employed a within-subject design in which each 
participant served as their own control. Experimental conditions were counterbalanced across trials where applicable.
